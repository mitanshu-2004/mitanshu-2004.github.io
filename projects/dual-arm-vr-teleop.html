Dual-arm VR teleoperation

2× Elite Robots CS66 125 Hz Cartesian servoing Quest 3, worn on the chest Real-time C++ Anchor/clutch control

Two arms, following my hands

Both arms follow my hand movements live, streamed from Meta Quest 3 controllers. Here they pass a block from one gripper to the other. Every motion is driven by me, in real time.

Built at Nferent AI. Two Elite CS66 arms, a block passed and stacked between their grippers, and no trajectory planned in advance — every motion here is mine, live.

The headset never goes on

Look at where the Quest 3 is: hanging on my chest, cameras pointing forward at my hands, while both arms track. It is never on my face.

Driving two industrial arms while wearing a headset is the easy version. It is also the version that gives you a sore neck in twenty minutes and hides the robot behind a video feed. So the rig runs with the headset off and worn as a chest-mounted tracking base — it still does the inside-out tracking that locates the controllers, and I keep my own eyes on the real arms. Operator comfort was the point; being able to watch the hardware directly rather than a stream turned out to matter more.

How it works

  • A real-time C++ loop maps controller poses to end-effector targets through SE(3) transforms, servoing in Cartesian space at 125 Hz. Hand pose arrives from the headset over UDP. One process per arm, so a stall on one never stutters the other.
  • A clutch lets me let go mid-task. Release to freeze the arms, move my hands somewhere comfortable, re-engage and continue. It works like lifting a mouse off the desk, and without it your arm ends up pinned against your body halfway through anything long.
  • Nothing I send reaches the hardware unchecked. A safety layer clamps the workspace, the end-effector velocity and the command rate first.
  • When the headset loses a controller it does not go quiet. It reports a plausible wrong pose, and on an industrial arm that pose becomes real motion, so the loop throws away frames it cannot trust.

What made it hard

Mapping a controller pose onto a 6-DOF arm takes an afternoon. Three things after that took the rest of the time.

A pose the arm can reach is not always a pose it can reach smoothly. Near a wrist singularity the inverse kinematics asks for enormous joint speeds to make a small Cartesian move, so the loop has to notice that and refuse rather than obey.

My hand also has no joint limits and no elbow to catch on a table, so I can put the controller somewhere the arm simply cannot follow. The arm stops cleanly at the edge of its workspace instead of tracking as far as it can and then jerking.

And the filtering has to sit in a narrow band. Too little and my hand tremor becomes arm tremor. Too much and the arm feels like it is moving underwater, which makes fine work impossible.

Where it goes

The rig exists to collect demonstrations. The same loop drives the Franka FR3, and the ten-task dataset it recorded is what a π0.5 policy was fine-tuned on. Every hesitation and correction in my hand ends up in that training data, so how well I teleoperate sets a ceiling on what the policy can learn.
